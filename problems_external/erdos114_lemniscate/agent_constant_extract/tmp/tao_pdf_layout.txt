                                                THE MAXIMAL LENGTH OF THE ERDŐS–HERZOG–PIRANIAN
                                                           LEMNISCATE IN HIGH DEGREE

                                                                                       TERENCE TAO

                                                  Abstract. Let n ≥ 1, and let p : C → C be a monic polynomial of degree n. It was
                                                  conjectured by Erdős, Herzog, and Piranian that the maximal length of lemniscate {z ∈
                                                  C : |p(z)| = 1} is attained by the polynomial p(z) = z n − 1. In this paper, building upon

                                                  a previous analysis of Fryntov and Nazarov, we establish this conjecture for all sufficiently
                                                  large n.




                                                                                     1. Introduction
                                           1.1. The Erdős–Herzog–Piranian conjecture. Let n ≥ 1, and let p : C → C be a monic
                                           polynomial of degree n. We introduce the regions
                                                                          ER = ER (p) := {z ∈ C : |p(z)| < R}
                                           for any R ≥ 0, with corresponding boundaries
                                                                         ∂ER = ∂ER (p) = {z ∈ C : |p(z)| = R}.
                                           We refer to ∂E1 (p) = {z ∈ C : |p(z)| = 1} as the Erdős–Herzog–Piranian lemniscate (or
                                           lemniscate for short) associated to p. The problem of bounding the arclength ℓ(∂E1 (p)) of
                                           this lemniscate was raised (amongst many other questions) by Erdős, Herzog, and Piranian
                                           in [6]. For instance, when n = 1, ∂E1 (p) is a unit circle and ℓ(∂E1 (p)) = 2π.
                                              As r → ∞, the lemniscates ∂E1 (z 2 − r2 ), which are Cassini ovals with foci ±r and
                                           eccentricity e = 1/r, can be calculated to have length O(1/r). Thus the lemniscate length
                                           can be arbitrarily small without further hypotheses; however, in [6, Problem 12] it was
                                           conjectured that when one also required E1 (p) to be connected, then one had the lower
                                           bound
                                                                          ℓ(∂E1 (p)) ≥ ℓ(∂E1 (z n )) = 2π.
                                           This was quickly verified by Pommerenke [17].
                                             The question of upper bounds for ℓ(∂E1 (p)) was more difficult, with the following conjec-
                                           ture raised in [6, Problem 12] (see also [7, p. 247], [8], [9], [10], [1, Problem 114]):
                                           Conjecture 1.1 (Erdős–Herzog–Piranian conjecture). If n ≥ 1, and p is monic of degree
                                           n, then
                                                                         ℓ(∂E1 (p)) ≤ ℓ(∂E1 (p0 ))
                                           where p0 (z) := z n − 1.

Remark 1.1. Note that the lemniscate length is unaffected if one replaces a polynomial p(z)
by a translate p(z − z0 ) or a rotation e−inθ p(eiθ z) for z0 ∈ C and θ ∈ R. Thus for instance
one could replace p0 in the above conjecture by any translated, rotated version (z − z0 )n − eiθ
of this polynomial (and it is natural to conjecture, especially in view of Theorem 1.1(iv)
below, that these are in fact the full set of maximizers of this length). To eliminate these
symmetries, we will find it convenient to work with polynomials p that are normalized in the
sense that the z n−1 coefficient vanishes and the constant coefficient is a non-positive real.
Then, p0 is the only remaining conjectural candidate for a maximizer.
  From the above discussion, Conjecture 1.1 holds for n = 1. To avoid minor degeneracies,
we will now assume n ≥ 2 henceforth. Numerical experimentation by the author using the
tool AlphaEvolve suggested that this conjecture was true for all n, as the tool quickly settled
on p0 (or a rotation and translation thereof) as a potential maximizer of ℓ(∂E1 (p)) for various
choices of degree n, though this fell well short of a rigorous proof.

1.2. Main results. Our main results sharpen the upper bounds, and in fact establish the
Erdős–Herzog–Piranian conjecture for sufficiently large n:
Theorem 1.1 (Main theorem). Let p be a monic polynomial of degree n.
                                      √
   (i) One has ℓ(∂E1 (p)) ≤ 2n + O( n).
  (ii) One has ℓ(∂E1 (p)) ≤ 2n + O(1).
 (iii) One has ℓ(∂E1 (p)) ≤ 2n + 4 log 2 + o(1) as n → ∞.
  (iv) If n is sufficiently large, then ℓ(∂E1 (p)) ≤ ℓ(∂E1 (p0 )). Furthermore, equality is
       attained if and only if p is equal to p0 up to rotation and translation (i.e., p(z) =
       (z − z0 )n − eiθ for some z0 ∈ C and θ ∈ R).
  Of course, each part of this theorem implies the previous ones, so one could retain only
the strongest conclusion (iv) and discard the other three components (i), (ii), (iii) of the
theorem; but our proof will be structured by establishing (i), (ii), (iii), and (iv) in turn, with
the proof of each part relying on the preceding ones. Part (iv) has some resemblance to a
previous result [19] of the author establishing Sendov’s conjecture for sufficiently high degree
polynomials, but the proof techniques here are rather different from those in that paper.
Remark 1.3. All implied constants in our arguments are effectively computable. Thus, in
view of Theorem 1.1(iv), the full verification of Conjecture 1.1 now reduces to checking the
conjecture for an explicitly bounded number of n, although we have made no attempt to op-
timize this bound. This almost allows us to declare this conjecture to be decidable; however,
there is still one (very unlikely) scenario which could obstruct this conjecture from being
decided in finite time, in which there is some bounded degree n and some competitor nor-
malized extremizer p1 ̸= p0 for which the lemniscate lengths ℓ(∂E1 (p0 )), ℓ(∂E1 (p1 )) happen
                                                4
to be identical. Due to the (likely) transcendental nature of these lengths, it is not imme-
diately obvious that one could decide between the rival scenarios ℓ(∂E1 (p0 )) < ℓ(∂E1 (p1 )),
ℓ(∂E1 (p0 )) = ℓ(∂E1 (p1 )), or ℓ(∂E1 (p0 )) > ℓ(∂E1 (p1 )) by a finite computation.
1.3. Methods of proof. We now discuss the methods of proof of Theorem 1.1. It will be
convenient to use the following result of Eremenko and Hayman [11]:
Proposition 1.2. Let n ≥ 1. Then there exists a monic polynomial p of degree n which
maximizes ℓ(∂E1 (p)) among all such polynomials. Furthermore, the lemniscate ∂E1 (p) is
connected and contains all the critical points of p. Finally, we can assume p to be normalized
in the sense of Remark 1.1.
Proof. The existence of a maximizing p whose lemniscate is connected and contains all of
its critical points follow from1 [11, Lemmas 5,6]. One can then replace p(z) by a translation
p(z −z0 ) so that the z n−1 coefficient vanishes, and then replace p(z) by a rotation e−inθ p(eiθ z)
so that p(0) is a non-positive real.                                                              □
    Let us refer to a polynomial p obeying all the properties of Proposition 1.2 as a normalized
maximizer. For instance, when n = 2 the only possible normalized maximizer is p0 (z) =
z 2 − 1, which explains why the n = 2 case of Conjecture 1.1 was already established in [11].
However, these properties do not uniquely identify the normalized maximizer for n ≥ 3; see
Figure 3.
    To prove Theorem 1.1, it clearly suffices to do so for normalized maximizers; in particu-
lar, (iv) is equivalent to the assertion that p0 is the only normalized maximizer when n is
sufficiently large.

  A guiding heuristic for our analysis is as follows:
Heuristic 1.1. For normalized polynomials p, the lemniscate length ℓ(∂E1 (p)) “behaves like”
ℓ(∂E1 (p0 )) − c∥p∥ for some constant c > 0. In particular:
    (i) Any increase in the dispersion ∥p∥1 is expected to induce a proportional decrease in
        the lemniscate length.
   (ii) Any increase in the origin repulsion ∥p∥0 is expected to induce a proportional decrease
        in the lemniscate length.
  (iii) As ℓ(∂E1 (p)) approaches ℓ(∂E1 (p0 )), both the dispersion ∥p∥1 and the origin repulsion
        ∥p∥0 are expected to approach zero, and the lemniscate ∂E1 (p) should increasingly
        resemble ∂E1 (p0 ) in shape.

and Ω) by a variety of tools, including classical area inequalities (see Section 4), rearrange-
ment inequalities (see Section 5), and partial fraction decompositions (see Section 2.4). It
is an easy matter to obtain Theorem 1.1(i) from Theorem 1.3 and the aforementioned tools
to control error terms; this can be viewed as an optimized      √ version of the arguments in [12]
(who, as mentioned previously, already identified the O( n), as a natural barrier to their
method). We carry out these arguments in Section 9.
   To proceed further, we analyze the defect in various inequalities used in the proof of
Theorem 1.1(i). It turns out that the most fruitful component inequality to analyze in this
fashion is the triangle inequality (1.20). One can use a defect version of this inequality
(see Section 7) to control the dispersion ∥p∥1 in terms of the lemniscate length ℓ(∂E1 (p)),
thus partially justifying Heuristic 1.1(i). This control on the dispersion in turn allows for
refined estimates for the error terms X1 , . . . , X5 , which ultimately leads to the improvement
Theorem 1.1(ii). We carry out these arguments in Section 10.
                                                 13
   To push beyond the O(1) error term in Theorem 1.1(ii) requires further analysis. The
previous arguments allow one to show that in all remaining cases, the dispersion ∥p∥1 is
bounded; elementary arguments (combined with another application of Theorem 1.3) also
permit one to show that the origin repulsion ∥p∥0 is also bounded; see Section 6. This
permits for further elementary pointwise estimates on p and p′ (again, see Section 6) that
are quite precise (especially in the interior portion of the disk D(0, 1)), and allow for very
accurate estimation of the lemniscate length ℓ(∂E1 (p)), both in interior, intermediate, and
outer portions of that disk by direct arclength calculations (and some tools for counting
zeroes of polynomials, such as Bezout’s theorem and Rouche’s theorem). This eventually
allows one to reduce the O(1) losses in lemniscate length estimates to O(ε) for any fixed
ε > 0, leading to the proof of Theorem 1.1(iii). We carry out these arguments in Section 11.
   The final part (iv) of Theorem 1.1 is achieved by a similar strategy. Firstly, the analysis
used to establish part (iii) is pushed further to show that the total size ∥p∥ is not merely
bounded, but is in fact small. A finer inspection of the various defect inequalities available
then shows that one can in fact improve the upper bound on lemniscate length by a small
multiple of ∥p∥, as per Heuristic 1.1; there are some losses incurred when doing so, but one
can contain these losses to be of size O(∥p∥/C0 ) or better for a large constant C0 , which lets
us show that ∥p∥ is not only small, but in fact vanishes. We carry out these arguments in
Section 12.

1.4. Organization of the paper. The proofs of the four components of Theorem 1.1 share
many common ingredients. In order to keep the paper at a manageable length, we have
therefore structured the paper to first introduce some general classes of tools for controlling
various aspects of the geometry of lemniscates, and then use these tools to establish each of
the four components of Theorem 1.1 in turn. More precisely:
     • In Section 2 we lay out our basic notation and easy observations.
     • In Section 3 we establish some formulae for the arclength of a lemniscate, and use
       this to obtain precise control on the standard lemniscate ∂E1 (p0 ).
     • In Section 4 we establish some area theorems that give reasonable control on the
       “roundness” of regions such as E2 (p) or E4 (p), as well as some preliminary L2 control
       on p′ .
     • In Section 5 we use rearrangement inequalities to control Riesz-type potentials, which
       are particularly useful to control the various expressions appearing in Theorem 1.3.
     • In Section 6 we establish some elementary pointwise estimates on p and p′ that are
       particularly useful in the regime when ∥p∥ is small.
     • In Section 7 we investigate the defect in the triangle inequality (1.20), and use this
       to obtain upper and lower bounds on Ψ(E) for various sets E.
     • In Section 8 we establish the crucial estimate, Theorem 1.3, through an application
       of Stokes’ theorem combined with an integration by parts argument.
     • In Section 9 we use the tools from the previous sections (particularly those from
       Sections 4, 5, 8) to give a very short proof of Theorem 1.1(i).
     • In Section 10 we use the tools from the previous sections (particularly those from
       Sections 4, 5, 7, 8) to obtain the improved bound of Theorem 1.1(ii).
     • In Section 11 we use the tools from all of the previous sections to give a (significantly
       more complicated) proof of the further improvement in Theorem 1.1(iii).
                                               14
      • Finally, in Section 12 we use the tools from all of the previous sections (and a repe-
        tition of the arguments in Section 11) to prove the final result, Theorem 1.1(iv).
