I do **not** have a valid proof of (A), (B), or (C). In particular, I found no way to turn the affine evidence into a positive-density certificate, and I found no argument forcing every finite subsystem to have density zero. Claiming either conclusion would require an unproved step.

What I can give is a rigorous obstruction that completely rules out two broad versions of route (A), together with a precise sufficient condition that isolates what a successful affine proof would still have to establish.

## 1. A universal obstruction to progression-cover arguments

Let (D) be any finite set of integers at least (2), and put

[
f_d(x)=dx-1 \qquad(d\in D).
]

For a nonempty word (w=(d_1,\ldots ,d_m)), write

[
F_w=f_{d_m}\circ\cdots\circ f_{d_1}.
]

### Proposition 1

Every (F_w) has the form

[
F_w(x)=P_wx-Q_w,
]

where

[
P_w=\prod_{j=1}^m d_j,\qquad 1\le Q_w\le P_w-1.
]

Moreover, if (d_m) is the outermost multiplier, then

[
Q_w\equiv 1\pmod {d_m},
]

and consequently

[
F_w(\mathbb Z)\subseteq
{n\in\mathbb Z:d_m\mid n+1}.
\tag{1}
]

#### Proof

Starting with (P=1,Q=0), outer composition by (f_d) gives

[
f_d(Px-Q)=dPx-(dQ+1).
]

Thus

[
P\longmapsto dP,\qquad Q\longmapsto dQ+1.
]

After the first step, (Q=1). If (1\le Q\le P-1), then

[
1\le dQ+1\le d(P-1)+1=dP-(d-1)\le dP-1.
]

This proves the first assertion inductively. At the final step,

[
Q_w=d_mQ'+1\equiv1\pmod {d_m},
]

while (d_m\mid P_w). Hence, for (n=P_wz-Q_w),

[
n+1\equiv -Q_w+1\equiv0\pmod {d_m}.
]

This proves (1). ∎

Let

[
R_D=\operatorname{rad}!\left(\operatorname{lcm}_{d\in D}d\right),
]

the product of the distinct primes dividing at least one member of (D).

### Corollary 2

For every collection (\mathcal W) of nonempty composition words,

[
\bigcup_{w\in\mathcal W}F_w(\mathbb Z)
\subseteq
{n:\gcd(n+1,R_D)>1}.
]

Consequently,

[
\overline d!\left(\bigcup_{w\in\mathcal W}F_w(\mathbb Z)\right)
\le
1-\frac{\varphi(R_D)}{R_D}<1.
\tag{2}
]

In particular, no finite or countable family of nonempty composition progressions can cover (\mathbb Z), even up to a density-zero exceptional set.

#### Proof

If (n\in F_w(\mathbb Z)), Proposition 1 gives a (d\in D) such that (d\mid n+1); hence some prime dividing (R_D) divides (n+1). On the other hand, the integers satisfying

[
\gcd(n+1,R_D)=1
]

have density (\varphi(R_D)/R_D). ∎

For (D={2,3,5}), this gives the explicit bound

[
\sum_{w\in\mathcal C}\frac1{P_w}
\le \frac{11}{15}
\tag{3}
]

for **every** pairwise-disjoint family of composition progressions ({F_w(\mathbb Z):w\in\mathcal C}), finite or countable. Indeed, finite partial sums equal the density of the corresponding finite disjoint union, and (2) bounds every partial sum.

Thus the observed (7471/10800) is not merely a depth limitation: reciprocal mass (1) is impossible for any disjoint progression code made from nonempty compositions of (f_2,f_3,f_5). The exact-cover theorem of Shamazov–Talambutsa therefore cannot be reached by passing to longer compositions. Their positive-density theorem genuinely uses a partition of (\mathbb Z), whereas their general critical free-semigroup theorem yields only the logarithmically deficient lower bound. ([[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.06875)][1])

## 2. The equal-slope escape is also impossible

### Proposition 3

Fix (P\ge2). There are at most (P-1) distinct composition maps of slope (P).

Equivalently, after conjugating by (x=y+1), every composition of slope (P) is

[
y\longmapsto Py+a
]

with

[
0\le a\le P-2.
\tag{4}
]

#### Proof

By Proposition 1, a map of slope (P) is (Px-Q) with (1\le Q\le P-1), giving at most (P-1) possibilities. Under (x=y+1),

[
Px-Q-1
======

Py+(P-Q-1),
]

and (P-Q-1\in{0,\ldots ,P-2}). ∎

Hence no one-state equal-slope subsystem obtained from these compositions can have the (P) distinct digits required for a critical base-(P) system. If it has (m\le P-1) maps, its depth-(k) orbit has at most (m^k) elements of size (O(P^k)), so its counting exponent is at most

[
\frac{\log m}{\log P}
\le
\frac{\log(P-1)}{\log P}<1.
]

Thus replacing the mixed-slope system by a finite critical equal-slope digit system cannot work either.

## 3. A precise affine criterion that would suffice

The following avoids progression covers entirely.

### Proposition 4 — fixed-slope saturation criterion

Let (D\subset A) be finite and let (s\in A) satisfy

[
s>\max D.
]

Suppose there are constants (c_0>0), (R<\infty), and slopes

[
P_1<P_2<\cdots,\qquad P_{k+1}\le RP_k,
]

such that, for every (k), the semigroup generated by the (f_d), (d\in D), contains at least (c_0P_k) distinct affine maps of slope exactly (P_k).

Then the affine orbit (S_D(s)) is contained in (A), all its generating steps respect distinct inputs, and

[
\liminf_{X\to\infty}\frac{|S_D(s)\cap[1,X]|}{X}
\ge \frac{c_0}{sR}.
\tag{5}
]

#### Proof

Every composition of slope (P_k) is (P_kx-Q), with (1\le Q\le P_k-1). Distinct maps of the same slope have distinct (Q), and therefore give distinct values at (s). Moreover,

[
P_k(s-1)+1
\le P_ks-Q
\le sP_k-1.
]

Consequently,

[
|S_D(s)\cap[1,sP_k]|\ge c_0P_k.
\tag{6}
]

For a sufficiently large (X), choose (k) with

[
sP_k\le X<sP_{k+1}.
]

Then (X<sRP_k), and (6) gives

[
\frac{|S_D(s)\cap[1,X]|}{X}
\ge
\frac{c_0P_k}{X}

>

\frac{c_0}{sR}.
]

Finally, if (x\ge s>\max D), then (x\ne d) for every (d\in D), and

[
f_d(x)-x=(d-1)x-1>0.
]

Thus all later orbit elements remain larger than every multiplier. Since (d,x\in A) and (d\ne x), every step (dx-1) is valid under the distinct-input convention. ∎

This criterion removes both difficulties that force the (X/(\log X)^c) bound in the general free-semigroup argument: maps of a common slope cannot collide at the seed, and no local-central-limit loss remains once one has (c_0P_k) maps at that slope.

## 4. An explicit valid subsystem, but with the decisive step still missing

There is a natural analytic candidate:

[
D={2,3,5,9,14},\qquad s=17.
]

All memberships and all preparatory steps use distinct inputs:

[
5=2\cdot3-1,\qquad
9=2\cdot5-1,\qquad
14=3\cdot5-1,\qquad
17=2\cdot9-1.
]

Thus (D\cup{17}\subset A), and (17>\max D).

Its inverse real maps

[
\phi_d(t)=\frac{t+1}{d}
]

cover the interval

[
I=\left[\frac1{13},1\right].
]

Indeed,

[
\begin{aligned}
\phi_{14}(I)&=\left[\frac1{13},\frac17\right],\
\phi_9(I)&=\left[\frac{14}{117},\frac29\right],\
\phi_5(I)&=\left[\frac{14}{65},\frac25\right],\
\phi_3(I)&=\left[\frac{14}{39},\frac23\right],\
\phi_2(I)&=\left[\frac7{13},1\right],
\end{aligned}
]

and successive intervals overlap. This removes any simple real-porosity argument for (B).

But it does **not** prove Proposition 4’s saturation hypothesis. Real interval covering only supplies symbolic inverse branches; it neither bounds the multiplicity of integer affine coefficients nor produces disjoint arithmetic cylinders. Treating it as a density proof would be precisely an unproved density bootstrap.

## Conclusion

The unconditional result is:

[
\boxed{\text{No disjoint progression code, finite or infinite, made from these compositions can have mass }1.}
]

More generally, all such codes have mass at most

[
1-\frac{\varphi(R_D)}{R_D},
]

and all one-state equal-slope reductions are strictly subcritical.

What remains unresolved is whether some explicit finite (D\subset A) satisfies the fixed-slope saturation condition of Proposition 4, or whether one can prove that such saturation—and every other positive-density mechanism—is impossible. The cited 2026 paper likewise leaves critical free affine examples outside the exact-cover setting unresolved. ([[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.06875v2)][2])

Therefore I cannot honestly designate the argument above as (A), (B), or (C): it rigorously eliminates two candidate mechanisms and isolates a sufficient replacement, but it does not decide the stated problem.
